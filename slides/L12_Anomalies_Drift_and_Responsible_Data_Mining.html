    01Part 1

    Anomaly Detection

    Recap from Lecture 3

    Noise vs Anomalies

    Outliers are data objects with characteristics that are considerably different than most of the other data objects in the data set. Today we look for them on purpose: they are called anomalies.

    Noise

    Noise in data could occur due to error in data collection, e.g., a student of age 123 years old (but is actually 23). Its presence would affect the accuracy of the results. It should be detected and removed.

    Noise example · data entry

    A cashier types a price of HK$1,500 instead of HK$15.00. The value is far from the rest, but it is a mistake, not a rare event: correct or remove it before mining.

    Noise example · sensors

    A thermometer that loses power logs −999 °C for an hour. These readings say nothing about the weather: drop them, or the average temperature is ruined.

    Noise is a wrong value: clean it (Lecture 3).

    Outliers

    Data that deviate so much from the norm that they may represent some very rare or exceptional cases (or just noise). Outliers are not necessarily noise: no one can ever guarantee that no one lives for more than 123 years. Outliers could be useful or not depending on the applications.

    Outlier example · credit cards

    Credit-card fraud detection relies a lot on outlier detection (exceptional cases, abnormal spending patterns) to discover possible cases of fraud.

    Outlier example · insurance

    An insurance company uses outlier detection to discover a fraudulent case in which a customer claims maternity compensation twice in half a year.

    A useful outlier is a real but rare case: investigate it (today).

    Outliers are not the norm. So, rules about them may or may not be useful. A flagged case is a lead for a person to check, not a verdict.

    Recap from Lecture 3

    Detecting Outliers

    Cluster analysis
    • By definition, outliers are data points that are not close to, or similar to, other data points in the data set.
    • A cluster is a group of data points that are similar among themselves.
    • Points that do not fall into any cluster could be flagged as outliers.
    Distance-based methods

    Simple kNN method: for a data object (point) p, the distance to its k-th nearest neighbor is taken as p's outlier score. The higher the score, the more likely that p is an outlier.

    Model-based methods

    Assume data follows certain model. Then, find data points that deviate from the model prediction.

    The next board writes these as scores: the z-score and IQR rules (model-based), the kNN distance, and a newer idea, local density (LOF). Isolation Forest follows.

    Three families of outlier scores

    Scoring Outliers: z-score, IQR, kNN Distance and LOF

    Each method turns a point into an outlier score: the higher, the more unusual. Rank by score and check the top k, or flag scores above a cut-off.

    Statistical (model-based)

    Model: each feature is one bell-shaped (normal) cloud.

    zj=xj−μjσj,score(x)=∑jzj2

    μj, σj: mean and sd of feature j. One feature: flag |z| > 3. Several: the distance from the mean in sd units.

    flagx<Q1−1.5·IQRorx>Q3+1.5·IQR

    Q1, Q3: 25th and 75th percentiles; IQR = Q3 − Q1. Unlike the mean and sd, quartiles barely move when outliers are added.

    Distance-based (Lecture 3)

    Far from everyone means unusual.

    score(p)=dk(p)

    dk(p): the distance from p to its k-th nearest neighbour. No model of the data needed.

    Weakness: one distance scale for the whole data set, so points of a sparse cluster score as high as true anomalies, and a point just outside a tight cluster scores low.

    Density-based: local outlier factor (LOF)

    Compare a point's local density with its neighbours' densities.

    lrd(p)=11k∑o∈Nk(p)max(dk(o),d(p,o))LOF(p)=1k∑o∈Nk(p)lrd(o)lrd(p)

    Nk(p): the k nearest neighbours of p; d(p, o): distance; dk(o): o's distance to its own k-th nearest neighbour. lrd: local reachability density, one over the average neighbour distance (each at least dk(o)). LOF ≈ 1: as dense as its neighbours; ≫ 1: much sparser.

    Isolation Forest · split it yourself

    Isolation Forest: Anomalies are Easy to Isolate

    Pick a feature at random and cut it at a random value. Repeat on the side that holds your point. An anomaly sits alone after a few cuts; a normal point needs many.

    Score

    Average the number of cuts h(x) over many random trees. Short paths mean anomalies: s(x) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(n), close to 1 for anomalies, below 0.5 for normal points. c(n) is the average path length for n points.

    Purple: the anomaly; grey: normal points. The ringed point is the one being isolated. Lines: the random cuts so far; the shaded box holds the points still with it.

    Evaluation · move the slider

    Evaluating Detectors when Anomalies are Rare

    1,000 card transactions, 10 of them fraud. A detector ranks all 1,000 by score; investigators check the top k. Accuracy is useless here: flagging nothing is already 99% accurate.

    The ranked list · ranks 1–100 of 1,000, two rows of 50 · filled = fraud · shaded = checked (top k)
    The PR curve · one point per k, from k = 1 (left) to k = 1,000 (right)
    Read it as

    Precision@k: share of the k checked cases that are fraud. Recall@k: share of all fraud found in the top k. Growing k walks along the curve: when a fraud enters the top k, recall steps right by 0.1 and precision rises (↗); when a normal case enters, precision falls (↓). A perfect detector stays at precision 1 until recall 1; a random one stays near 0.01.

    Synthetic scores, generated in your browser: fraud cases score higher on average, but the two groups overlap.

    02Part 2

    Drift

    When the world changes

    Data Drift and Concept Drift

    A model is trained on yesterday's data and used on today's. Two different things can change.

    Data drift · P(x) changes

    The inputs look different

    New kinds of customers, a new product mix, a sensor replaced. The rule from inputs to outcome may still hold, but the model now works where it saw little training data.

    Detect without labels: compare input distributions between windows.

    Concept drift · P(y | x) changes

    The same inputs mean something else

    Fraudsters change tactics; a policy changes who repays a loan. The inputs can look exactly as before while the right answer has moved.

    Detect with labels: watch accuracy, or proxies such as confidence, as labels arrive.

    Watch the inputs

    Each feature's distribution, this month against an earlier period.

    Watch the outputs

    Share of positive predictions, and mean confidence.

    Watch the outcomes

    Accuracy as labels arrive, overall and per slice.

    Responsible Data Mining

    Recap from Lecture 1

    Privacy and Fair Information Practice

    Challenges in data mining: legal challenges, and the results themselves.

    Ownership

    Data ownership and distribution: who may use the data, and who may pass it on?

    Privacy

    Privacy protection and regulations, e.g., the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and in Hong Kong the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (PDPO).

    Bias

    Algorithmic biases: a model can treat groups of people differently.

    Results

    Interpretation of patterns, and explainability: can we say why a pattern or a prediction came out?

    Fair information practice

    "The primary purpose of the collection must be clearly understood by the consumer and identified at the time of the collection. Data mining, however, is a secondary, future use. As such it requires the explicit consent of the data subjects."

    Today: what "anonymous" really means, the law in Hong Kong, fair treatment of groups, and what a trained model can leak.

    Two words that are often confused

    Anonymisation vs Pseudonymisation

    One clinic record, three ways to release it.

    releasenamebirth yeardistrictsexdiagnosis
    RawChan Mei Ling1987Sha TinFasthma
    PseudonymisedP-44711987Sha TinFasthma
    Anonymised–1980–89New Territories–asthma
    Pseudonymisation

    Replace direct identifiers with a code. Someone holds the key, and the other columns still describe one person. It is still personal data: it lowers the risk but does not remove it.

    Anonymisation

    Change the data so that no one can be singled out, even by linking it with other data. Hard to achieve, and it costs detail: check it, never assume it.

    A made-up record.

    Re-identification join · generalise the release

    Re-identification by Linking, and k-Anonymity

    Removing names is not enough. A clinic releases records without names; a sports club's public member list has names, birth years, districts and sex. Join the two on those columns: a record that matches exactly one member is re-identified, and that person's diagnosis is exposed.

    k-anonymity

    Quasi-identifiers: columns that are not names but, combined, can single a person out (here birth year, district, sex). A release is k-anonymous if every combination of quasi-identifiers is shared by at least k records. Then a linked name points to at least k people, not one. The defence: generalise the quasi-identifiers until k is large enough, at the cost of detail.

    Red rows: re-identified, exactly one club member matches. "Club matches" counts the members who match a record. 12 made-up clinic records and a made-up member list of 40 people.

    Law and guidance · Hong Kong

    The PDPO and its Six Data Protection Principles

    The Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486) sets six data protection principles (DPPs). Read them as a data miner's obligations.

    DPP1

    Data Collection

    Collect lawfully and fairly, only what is needed, and tell people the purpose.

    DPP2

    Accuracy and Retention

    Keep the data accurate, and no longer than the purpose needs.

    DPP3

    Data Use

    Use the data for the purpose it was collected for, or a directly related one. A new purpose needs the person's consent. This is where secondary mining sits.

    DPP4

    Data Security

    Protect the data against unauthorised or accidental access, processing, loss or use.

    DPP5

    Openness

    Make your policies and practices on personal data available.

    DPP6

    Data Access and Correction

    People may ask for their data and ask to correct it.

    Covered as obligations for a data mining project, not legal advice. For a real project, check the Ordinance and the Privacy Commissioner's guidance, or ask a lawyer.

    Fairness explorer · move the thresholds

    Group Fairness: Equal Rates and Equal Errors

    A model scores loan applicants in groups A and B, and approves scores above each group's threshold. Base rate (share who would repay): 50% in A, 30% in B. Two definitions of fair:

    Equal approval rates (demographic parity)
    P(approved|A)=P(approved|B)

    Both groups get loans at the same rate.

    Equal error rates (equalised odds)
    TPRA=TPRB,FPRA=FPRB

    TPR: share of would-repay applicants approved. FPR: share of would-default applicants approved. The model is equally accurate for both groups.

    Plot: score histograms per group; purple = would repay, grey = would default. Applicants right of the black line are approved. Both groups' scores follow the same rule, only the base rate differs. 2,000 synthetic applicants per group, generated in your browser.

    Leakage from models · attack it

    Membership Inference and Memorisation

    Membership inference: from a model's outputs alone, guess whether a person's record was in its training data (a member). If the data is "patients with condition X", membership itself is sensitive.

    The attack
    1. Submit the person's record to the model.
    2. Read its confidence: its probability for the true label.
    3. Guess "member" if the confidence is above a threshold.
    Why it works: memorisation

    An overfit model has partly memorised its training records: near-certain on them, less sure on new ones. Large language models can repeat a rare training string, such as a phone number.

    Mitigations
    • Overfit less: regularise, stop training early.
    • Return only the predicted label, not the confidence.
    • Differential privacy: add calibrated noise in training, so no single record changes the model much.
    • Remove duplicates and secrets (phone numbers, IDs) before training.

    400 training records (members) and 400 unseen records (non-members). Overfit: a random forest of fully grown trees; regularised: shallower trees. Attack accuracy: the average of members caught and non-members correctly passed (0.50 = guessing).

    04Part 4 · AI layer

    Agentic Data Science

    AI as a tool

    Agents Doing Data Science

    An agent plans, writes code, runs it, reads the output and tries again, with no person between the steps. Benchmarks measure how far that gets.

    Benchmark · MLE-bench

    75 machine-learning competitions from Kaggle, each with its data, a task and a leaderboard. In the paper (Chan et al., 2025), the best agent set-up reached at least a bronze-medal score in 16.9% of the competitions.

    Agents do well
    • Boilerplate: loading, joining, plotting.
    • Trying many models and settings fast.
    • Writing a tidy first report.
    Agents fail
    • Leaky features and random splits of time-ordered data.
    • Rows dropped without saying so.
    • Metrics that do not fit the task, such as accuracy on rare fraud.
    • Claims with no table or test behind them.
